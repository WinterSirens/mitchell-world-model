# Crucial Conversations Reference

Pull this in when Mitchell is preparing for a high-stakes conversation: difficult feedback, scope pushback, role asks, escalations, boundary-setting, conflict, or any conversation where emotions run strong, stakes are high, and opinions vary.

## The STATE Method

Use STATE when sharing your perspective in a high-stakes conversation. The order matters.

**1. Share your facts.** Start with the least controversial, most objective observations. Facts are hard to dispute.

Right: "The feature was delivered Friday at 11 AM when we agreed on Thursday at 2 PM."
Wrong: "You don't prioritize our deadlines."

**2. Tell your story.** Share your interpretation tentatively, as a perspective, not a verdict.

Use softening phrases:
- "I'm beginning to wonder if..."
- "The story I'm telling myself is..."
- "I'm concerned this might..."
- "In my opinion..."

**3. Ask for their path.** Genuinely invite their facts, story, and feelings.

- "What's your take on this?"
- "How do you see it?"
- "Help me understand what happened."
- "What am I missing?"

**4. Talk tentatively.** Be clear about your view while staying open to theirs. Confidence with humility.

**5. Encourage testing.** Make it explicit that you want to hear opposing views.

- "Please let me know if you see this differently."
- "What am I missing from your perspective?"
- "Push back on this if it feels off."

## Creating Safety

People engage in difficult conversations when two conditions exist:

**Mutual purpose.** They believe you care about their goals, not just yours.

**Mutual respect.** They believe you value them as a person.

When safety breaks (the conversation goes silent or hostile), restore it before continuing.

### Contrasting Statements

Use contrasting statements to clarify what you do and don't mean. They restore safety fast.

**Format:** "I don't want X. I do want Y."

Examples:
- "I don't want to question your commitment to the project. I do want to find ways to help you succeed."
- "I don't want this to feel like a personal critique. I do want us to figure out how to prevent it next time."
- "I don't want to slow your work down. I do want to make sure we're aligned before you sink more time in."

## Recognizing When a Conversation Becomes Crucial

Three markers:
1. Opinions vary
2. Stakes are high
3. Emotions run strong

When you notice these, shift from content focus to process management. Slow down. Create safety. Don't argue the point yet.

### Warning Signs in Yourself

- Physical: stomach tightening, racing heartbeat, sudden body tension
- Emotional: sudden shift to anger, fear, or frustration
- Behavioral: wanting to win the argument, wanting to escape, wanting to punish

### Warning Signs in Others

**Moving to silence:** withdrawing, avoiding, masking with sarcasm, going quiet, becoming non-responsive.

**Moving to violence:** controlling the conversation, labeling, attacking, becoming overly aggressive, dominating airtime.

When you see either, pause and rebuild safety before continuing.

## Managing Your Own Emotions: Path to Action

The model: See/Hear → Tell Story → Feel → Act.

The insight: you control the story you tell yourself about what happened. The story drives the emotion. The emotion drives the action.

When you notice yourself becoming reactive, retrace the path:
1. What am I doing? (the action)
2. What am I feeling? (the emotion)
3. What story am I telling myself? (the interpretation)
4. What facts do I actually have? (separate facts from story)
5. What's a more complete story? (consider other interpretations)

### Three Clever Stories That Sabotage Dialogue

These stories remove your responsibility and lock you into a defensive position. Watch for them.

**Victim story:** "It's not my fault." Focuses on others' actions, ignores your contribution.

**Villain story:** "It's all their fault." Exaggerates others' bad motives. Assumes the worst.

**Helpless story:** "There's nothing I can do." Convinces you that you have no options.

For Mitchell specifically: when dealing with scope creep, role friction, or stakeholder pushback, watch for villain stories ("they're trying to block me") and helpless stories ("there's no way to make this work"). The story is usually the problem before the situation is.

## Influence Without Authority

Cross-functional work and AI consulting at MX both require influence without formal authority. Use questions to invite collaboration rather than triggering resistance.

**Replace demands with questions:**

Instead of: "I need your team to prioritize this feature."
Try: "What would need to happen for this feature to align with your current priorities?"

Instead of: "We have to fix this by end of quarter."
Try: "What would it take to land this by end of quarter? What's the biggest blocker?"

**Connect to their wins.** Frame requests around their team's success metrics and goals, not yours.

**Upward communication.** When talking with leadership:
- Focus on business impact, not personal frustration
- Come with solutions, not just problems
- Align with their known priorities
- Lead with: "I have some observations about [X] that could help us achieve [shared goal]"

## CRIB: Recovering When the Conversation Goes Sideways

When tension rises and you're losing alignment, use CRIB:

**C**ommit to mutual purpose. Out loud, name the shared goal you both care about.

**R**ecognize the purpose behind their strategy. Understand what they're trying to protect or achieve.

**I**nvent a higher-level mutual purpose. Find a goal that includes both of your underlying purposes.

**B**rainstorm new strategies. Together, generate options that serve the shared purpose.

Quick recovery line: "I can see we're both getting defensive. Can we take a breath and refocus on our shared goal?"

## Follow-Through: WWWF

Good conversations turn into action through WWWF:

- **W**ho does
- **W**hat by
- **W**hen, with
- **F**ollow-up

Without this, even the best conversation goes nowhere. Always close difficult conversations with a clear who/what/when and a follow-up commitment.

## Common Project Management Crucial Conversations

Five conversations that determine project success:

1. **Broken promises and missed deadlines.** Lead with facts, ask for their path, identify systemic causes before assigning blame.

2. **Insufficient resources and unrealistic timelines.** Frame as risk to shared goals. Bring data on what's possible vs. what's been requested.

3. **Scope creep and changing priorities.** Establish mutual purpose around project success first. Then surface the tradeoffs.

4. **Violated expectations and mismatched assumptions.** Surface the gap between what you thought was agreed and what's happening. Don't accuse.

5. **Crucial feedback and performance issues.** Specific behavioral facts first, then impact, then invite their perspective.

## Patterns for Specific Situations

### Delivering difficult feedback

"The [specific behavior/output] was [observed fact]. I'm concerned this might affect [specific outcome]. Help me understand what happened and how we can prevent this going forward."

### Pushing back on scope

"I want to make sure I understand what you're going for. The current scope includes [X]. Adding [Y] would mean [tradeoff]. What's most important to you here?"

### Setting work-life boundaries

"I want to be responsive to urgent needs while maintaining work-life balance. I'll [specific commitment for urgent matters]. For non-urgent, I'll [specific commitment]. Does this work for you?"

### Asking for a role or change

Don't pitch. Invite. Lead with their problem before your ask. Use the language of "What would need to be true for [outcome] to make sense?" Make it feel like a no-brainer investment, not a personal promotion.

### Escalation messages

Lead with the impact. Specific numbers. Specific time. Specific affected users or revenue. Then state what's been tried. Then state the ask.
