# Cialdini's Influence Principles Reference

Pull this in when Mitchell is writing something explicitly persuasive: stakeholder pitches, role proposals, sales-adjacent outreach, audience-building, course launch copy, or any communication where ethical influence techniques would help.

## Ethics First: The FAIR Test

Before applying any principle, run the FAIR test:

- **F**acts: Is the information accurate and complete? No deception, no exaggeration.
- **A**ccess: Are motives transparent? Would the recipient feel respected if they saw your reasoning?
- **I**mpacts: Considered effects on all stakeholders, not just the desired outcome?
- **R**espect: Honoring the recipient's autonomy and right to choose?

If any FAIR check fails, rework the approach. Manipulation and ethical influence look similar from the outside but feel completely different to the recipient over time.

## The Seven Principles

### 1. Reciprocity

**Mechanism:** People feel compelled to return favors, gifts, or services they receive, especially when the initial offering is personalized, unexpected, and unrequested.

**For Mitchell:** Provide value before asking. This works especially well with stakeholders, executives, and AI PM network contacts.

**Language patterns:**
- "Before we talk about what I need, let me share..."
- "I put together [resource] that I thought you'd find useful."
- "Here's something I built that solves [their problem]..."
- "I'd like to share something I've been working on that might help..."

**Application examples:**
- Send a stakeholder the analysis or dashboard they need before asking for their support on a role
- Offer to red-team or audit a teammate's project before asking them to back your initiative
- Share specific, valuable insights from your AI consulting work before asking for budget conversations

### 2. Commitment and Consistency

**Mechanism:** People feel internal and social pressure to behave consistently with their previous commitments and stated values.

**For Mitchell:** Surface a stakeholder's existing commitments before making an ask. Reference what they've already said they value.

**Language patterns:**
- "Based on what you've told me about [priority]..."
- "Given your commitment to [goal]..."
- "You mentioned [X] is important. This work directly supports that..."
- "Last time we talked, you said [Y]. I've been thinking about how to make that real..."

**Application examples:**
- When pitching the AI PM role, reference the org's stated priority on AI strategy
- When asking for budget, surface the leader's previous commitment to AI tooling investment
- For audience building, get small commitments first (newsletter signup) before bigger asks (workshop enrollment)

**Yes ladder:** Structure conversations to get small agreements before larger requests. Each "yes" makes the next one more likely.

### 3. Social Proof

**Mechanism:** People determine correct behavior by observing what similar others are doing. Strongest when the comparison group is uncertain and similar.

**For Mitchell:** Use specific numbers and similar-peer references. "Your peers" beats "people in general."

**Language patterns:**
- "The majority of [similar role] have..."
- "Companies like [comparable company] are already..."
- "Other [role title] in [their industry] typically..."
- "Most teams I've worked with on this end up..."

**Application examples:**
- For AI PM positioning, reference similar roles at fintech competitors
- For workshop launches, share specific student counts or testimonials from similar professionals
- For internal proposals, reference what other teams have already done with AI tooling

**Be specific.** "44% of guests reuse towels" beats "many guests reuse towels." Numbers create real social proof.

### 4. Authority

**Mechanism:** People defer to perceived experts to reduce decision-making effort. Symbols, titles, and third-party endorsements often work as well as actual expertise.

**For Mitchell:** Authority is built, not claimed. Third-party introductions of credentials beat self-promotion.

**Language patterns:**
- "Studies from [institution] demonstrate..."
- "Industry experts recommend..."
- "Data from [credible source] indicates..."
- "[Trusted person] suggested I reach out about this..."

**Application examples:**
- Have stakeholders introduce your work to others rather than self-promoting
- Reference the CPMAI certification, Google Generative AI cert, and AI PM bootcamp when relevant
- Build case studies from internal MX work to use as authority anchors
- For external audience: name-drop Jeff Walker / PLF community when relevant

**Critical:** Authority claims must be real and verifiable. Hollow authority claims destroy trust permanently.

### 5. Liking

**Mechanism:** People are more easily influenced by those they like. Liking is built on similarity, sincere compliments, attractive qualities, cooperation, and positive association.

**For Mitchell:** Build genuine connection before business discussions. Find real common ground.

**Language patterns:**
- "I can see we share similar values around..."
- "You have an excellent point about..."
- "I appreciate your perspective on..."
- "I noticed we both care about..."

**Application examples:**
- Personal connection before business asks (especially with leadership)
- Find genuine commonalities, not manufactured ones
- Compliment specific work or thinking, not generic qualities
- Show your humanity in stakeholder conversations, not just competence

**Research point:** MBA negotiation studies showed groups that exchanged personal information and found similarities reached 90% agreement vs. 55% for task-focused groups.

### 6. Scarcity

**Mechanism:** People place higher value on things that are rare or becoming less available. Loss aversion is stronger than gain seeking.

**For Mitchell:** Use real scarcity, never manufactured. Frame what they stand to lose, not just gain.

**Language patterns:**
- "This window closes when..."
- "The opportunity is unique because..."
- "If we don't move on this, we lose..."
- "Limited spots / Limited time" (only when actually true)

**Application examples:**
- For workshop launches with Mary Lee, real cohort limits create real scarcity
- For internal AI tooling pitches, frame the cost of waiting (competitor advances, lost productivity)
- For role asks, position the opportunity timing (organizational momentum, current needs)

**Critical:** Manufactured scarcity destroys trust. Only use scarcity that's actually true.

**Loss framing beats gain framing.** "You'll lose the ability to do X" lands harder than "You'll gain the ability to do X" for the same content.

### 7. Unity

**Mechanism:** People are most influenced by those they consider "one of us." Unity transcends mere liking. It's shared identity, group membership, collective experience.

**For Mitchell:** Unity is more powerful than individual rapport. Shared identity creates the strongest influence.

**Language patterns:**
- "As fellow [shared identity]..."
- "People like us understand..."
- "Our community has found..."
- "Folks who've been through [shared experience] know..."

**Critical distinction:** Ask for "advice" (co-creation, unity) instead of "opinions" (external feedback, distance). The word choice changes the relationship.

**Application examples:**
- For PLF community course launches, lean into "fellow practitioners" framing
- For AI PM positioning at MX, build identity around "people building the future of AI here"
- For audience building, create shared identity around the AI SHIFT method or strategic AI use

**Warren Buffett pattern:** "I will tell you what I would say to my family today." This creates unity by including the listener in the inner circle.

## Combination Strategies

Single principles work. Combinations work better. Three combinations to know:

### Trust Triangle (Authority + Liking + Reciprocity)

**Use for:** Initial relationship building. New stakeholders, new audience members, first conversations with potential allies.

**How:** Establish credibility through expertise (Authority), build genuine rapport (Liking), provide value first (Reciprocity).

### Urgency Stack (Scarcity + Social Proof + Consistency)

**Use for:** Closing situations. Course enrollments, role asks at decision time, project approvals at sprint planning.

**How:** Show real time pressure (Scarcity), demonstrate similar peers acting (Social Proof), reference their previous commitments (Consistency).

### Belonging Bridge (Unity + Liking + Social Proof)

**Use for:** Community building. Audience growth, internal coalition building, course community development.

**How:** Emphasize shared identity (Unity), build personal connection (Liking), show peer acceptance (Social Proof).

## Sequential Application

For longer communications (proposals, pitches, sales pages), sequence principles by phase:

**Phase 1: Opening (Liking + Authority)**
Build rapport through genuine commonalities. Establish credibility through relevant expertise or third-party intro.

**Phase 2: Discovery (Social Proof + Scarcity)**
Share relevant case studies or similar-peer success. Surface real urgency or limited windows.

**Phase 3: Presentation (Reciprocity + Consistency)**
Offer real value upfront. Reference their stated priorities and commitments.

**Phase 4: Closing (Unity + Scarcity)**
Frame as shared identity moving forward together. Make the timing or scarcity concrete.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**One-size-fits-all.** Different audiences need different principle combinations. Tailor.

**Skipping relationship building.** Going straight to influence without establishing connection feels manipulative.

**Over-relying on logic.** Logical appeals miss the emotional and social drivers of decisions.

**Ignoring counterarguments.** Address concerns proactively. Don't pretend they don't exist.

**Manufactured scarcity or false authority.** These destroy trust permanently. Real or nothing.

## When NOT to Use These Principles

- Personal letters to friends and family (use the voice guide directly)
- Routine logistical messages
- Internal team status updates
- Anything where the recipient's autonomy is the point

These principles are tools for ethical influence in contexts where influence is appropriate. Not every communication is a persuasion situation.
